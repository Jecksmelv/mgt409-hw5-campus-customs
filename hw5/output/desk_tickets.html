The short version

The team got all three tickets to a sensible place, and nothing moved money without me clicking Approve. The guardrails I cared most about held every time: no purchase order went to a vendor with an open invoice, no lead time was invented, and cash never went below zero. The weak spot wasn't safety. It was coordination. The one real failure (ticket 101, run 2) happened because the Boss read a paraphrase of the state instead of the state itself and drew the wrong conclusion. That was the most interesting thing I learned from the whole build.

1–3. Ticket by ticket

Ticket 101: the tee that took three runs

What worked. Run 1 was almost exactly the plan I wrote in Problem 6:

  • The Boss sent it to Inventory first.
  • Inventory found 0 of size S, matched apparel to Bulldog Print Co, read lead_days = 5, and saw can_ship = false because of invoice 501.
  • Inventory then handed off to Accounting itself, which is the one place an agent other than the Boss started a hand-off. Accounting queued invoice 501 for $840 and, importantly, did not try to order the tee.
  • Customer Service drafted a reply to Tauhid.

That is the "pay the vendor before it ships" rule working the way a person would handle it. One honest caveat: run 1 finished in 11 seconds because Portkey had cached replies from an earlier identical test, so the model's words were replayed. The tool calls still ran live against the reset database. I'd still count it, but I'm not going to pretend it was a fresh run.

What broke. After I approved #1, I re-ran 101 so the team could order the tee. Run 2 called Inventory, which correctly reported can_ship = true, and then the Boss skipped Accounting entirely. Its reasoning was that an "approved" request "does not establish payment execution." But the approval row had a payment_id, and payments had the $840 row. The facts were all there. The Boss just didn't have a rule for reading them, and nothing in its prompt said "if the vendor can ship and there's a shortage, get Accounting to order it." The prompt only covered the blocked case. So the Boss stopped, marked the ticket in_progress, and saved another draft.

The fix and run 3. I added two lines to the Boss and Accounting prompts: order exactly the shortage once the vendor can ship, and approved plus a payment_id means money moved. In run 3, Accounting confirmed payment #1 and created PO #2 for 1 × CC-TEE-WHITE S at the $8.00 unit cost. I approved it, so the tee arrives around 2026-09-05. That's honest, because it's computed from the shop date plus the vendor's lead days, not guessed.

Expected vs actual.

  • Matched: Inventory went first, Facilities stayed on the bench, there was no PO while 501 was open, and the customer draft gave no firm date.
  • Missed: Inventory never called list_inventory, so none of the three drafts offered Tauhid the M (5), L (3) or XL (2) tees, which was literally in my plan.
  • Clunky: the board now has three nearly identical drafts for Tauhid (#1–#3), and nothing marks #1 and #2 as superseded.
  • Small leak: draft #2 tells the customer "our restock vendor is now able to ship," which hints at the vendor situation even though it doesn't name the invoice.

Approvals. Two, and they were the workflow:

  • #1 ($840) turned 101 from "blocked" into "orderable." Without it, nothing downstream could happen.
  • #2 ($8) committed the actual order.

The ticket said "resolved" on the board while #1 was still pending. That's my desk rule ("a clean run marks the ticket resolved") clashing with the Boss's own status (waiting_approval). The saffron ribbon covered for it visually, but the two statuses mean different things, and that's a design debt I created.

Ticket 102: the cleanest one

What worked. Everything.

  • The Boss went to Facilities.
  • Facilities ran get_rent_status and confirmed $2,400 due 2026-09-02, days_until_due = 2, is_overdue = false. That was measured against desk.date_today, not the real October date (the first smoke-test session in Problem 4 literally tripped on that).
  • Facilities checked list_approvals for a duplicate, then handed off to Accounting.
  • Accounting checked cash and queued #3. The amount came from leases.monthly_rent, not from anything an agent typed.

13 model calls, about 31k tokens: the cheapest ticket by far.

Expected vs actual. An exact match on the hand-offs (Boss → Facilities → Accounting) and on the tools I listed. The optional landlord draft didn't happen, which is fine. One small thing I noticed: the Boss's final call lists agents_consulted: ["facilities"], even though Accounting did the actual work. The Boss only sees who it delegated to, not who they called. In a bigger system, that's how a manager loses track of who touched what. The audit trail gets it right; the Boss's own summary doesn't.

Approval. #3 for $2,400 was the only thing left after the run. Approving it moved cash from $2,552 to $152 and moved leases.next_due to 2026-10-02 in the same transaction. The rest of the workflow didn't depend on it, but it set up ticket 103's outcome.

Ticket 103: good judgment, a little narrow

What worked.

  • Inventory found 8 of 20 size M (short 12) and confirmed Bulldog Print Co could now ship.
  • Accounting priced the deal at 10%, 15% and 20% off with quote_bulk_order, worked out that 12 × $22 = $264 of restock against $152 of cash, and didn't even attempt the PO.
  • The Boss chose 10% off ($52.20, 57.9% margin, $604 margin on 20) and a split fulfillment.
  • Customer Service drafted #4 to the Yale AI Club without mentioning costs, cash or invoices.

Declining to spend money it doesn't have, without being told, is the behaviour I actually want from an operations team.

What didn't.

  • The discount is the model's call, and it isn't stable. Across the three times ticket 103 has been run (two Problem 5 tests and this full run), the Boss picked 10%, then 15%, then 10% for the same ticket. Both are defensible, but a real shop would want a discount policy, not a coin flip.
  • Nobody looked sideways. There are 14 navy hoodies in size L and 6 in XL. A student org ordering 20 hoodies might take some L's to make the order now, and no agent considered it, because Inventory never called list_inventory. Same blind spot as 101.
  • The guardrail was never tested live. Because Accounting reasoned its way out of the PO, the server-side refusal ("cash can't cover this") never fired in the full run. That's good behaviour, but it means the code only proved itself in my Problem 5 and Problem 7 tests.

Expected vs actual. The hand-offs matched (Inventory, then Accounting, then the Boss decides, then Customer Service), and the discount landed inside my 10–15% range. The interesting difference: my plan said the restock would be blocked by invoice 501. By the time 103 ran, I'd already paid 501 on ticket 101, so the blocker became cash. I predicted $160 left; the real figure was $152, because of the $8 tee order. The plan reasoned about each ticket in isolation, but the real run was sequential, and each approval changed the world for the next ticket. If I'd rejected the rent, Accounting would have seen $2,552 and could have ordered the hoodies.

Approval. None was needed, because no request was created. The approval that mattered for 103 was #3 on ticket 102: my rent click is what made the hoodie restock unaffordable. And the ticket shows "resolved," although the Boss said in_progress and the club hasn't replied. "Resolved" in this app really means "the team finished its turn," not "the customer is happy."

Cash, in one breath

$3,400.00 − $840.00 (invoice 501) − $8.00 (tee PO) − $2,400.00 (rent) = $152.00, which matches cash_accounts exactly. The money went where it should: an overdue bill that was blocking a customer, the lease that keeps the doors open, and an $8 order for an actual customer. What worries me is the next step: there's no revenue model, $152 in the bank, and the October rent of $2,400 is due 2026-10-02. That's the cliff the shop is walking toward, and the agents can see it but can't do anything about it.

4. Where one agent with tools would have been simpler

  • Ticket 102 was a straight line. Read the lease, check the cash, queue the rent. Facilities' whole job was one lookup and a hand-off to Accounting, and that hop cost an extra agent loop, an extra get_ticket, and a brief the next agent had to re-read. One agent holding get_rent_status and request_payment would do it in about four calls.
  • The "pay first, then order" chain on ticket 101 is mostly rules. Stock check → vendor check → if blocked, pay the invoice; if not, order the shortage. That's a decision tree, not a negotiation. Splitting it across Inventory, Accounting and the Boss is exactly what created the run-2 failure: the Boss acted on a summary of Accounting's world instead of reading payments itself. One agent with all the tools would have seen the payment_id in the same context and moved on.
  • Repeated context was the biggest token cost. Every delegated agent re-read the ticket (Inventory, Accounting and Customer Service each called get_ticket in 101 run 1), and the Boss and Accounting both called get_cash_position. Ticket 103 used about 65k tokens. A single agent would probably have used a third of that.
  • Most guardrails don't come from the agent split. No negative cash, no PO to a blocked vendor, human-only payments and drafts-only outreach all live in mcp_server/server.py. They'd protect a single agent just as well.

Where the team did earn its keep:

  • Customer Service having its own prompt and only customer-facing tools is a big reason the drafts didn't leak cost or margin numbers. One agent holding both quote_bulk_order and save_draft would be more likely to slip "our margin is still 57%" into a customer email.
  • The roster view in the dashboard (Facilities dimmed on 101 and 103, Inventory and Customer Service benched on 102) makes the routing legible to me as the human. That matters for trusting the system.

So my honest take: for these three tickets, a single agent with role-scoped tool sets would have been simpler and cheaper. The multi-agent setup pays off for separating customer voice from money decisions, and probably once ticket volume and variety grow.

What I'd change next

  • Separate "team done" from "ticket done." Keep the Boss's status (waiting_approval, in_progress) as the real ticket status, and show "team finished" as its own badge. Tickets 101 and 103 said resolved while there was still real work left.
  • Make list_inventory part of every shortage check, so the team offers alternatives (Tauhid's M/L/XL, the club's L hoodies).
  • Supersede old drafts when Customer Service writes a new one for the same ticket.
  • Give the Boss list_approvals with payment details in its own context, or let it read payments directly, so it never has to trust a paraphrase about whether money moved.
  • Put a discount policy in the database (for example, a max % by quantity) so ticket 103 doesn't get 10% one run and 15% the next.
